There is a jug sitting in your kitchen right now that is about to go out with the trash. It is a gallon jug. It is watertight. It has a handle molded into the side of it and a cap that seals and it cost somebody money to make and it will hold anything you put in it without leaking a drop. And you are going to walk it out to the bin because it is empty and because that is what everybody does with them. I want you to stop and look at it a moment before you do. Because if you handed that jug to my grandmother, a watertight vessel with a handle and a lid, she would have looked at you like you had lost your senses for suggesting it be thrown away. In her kitchen, a thing like that had a job before the day was out. I am Elias Yoder. I farm in Lancaster County, Pennsylvania. I want to show you five things I do with those jugs on this place. And every one of them saves you buying something the garden store would happily sell you. Now, somebody is going to ask, so let me answer it plainly before we go a step further. Yes, we have these jugs, plenty of them. This is not a thing we make, and it is not a thing my grandfather would have recognized, and I will not pretend otherwise. But they come into a house ours the same as yours. They come home from the store. They come back from a gathering. Neighbors set them by the door when they know I want them. And I have been given whole sacks of them by folks who thought it was a strange thing to want. And a man who throws away a good watertight vessel because it happens to be modern has not been paying attention. It is not where the thing came from. It is whether it can still do work. None of this thinking came from me plainly. It came from a shelf in a kitchen I grew up in. And I will tell you about that shelf in a moment. So I wrote down what it taught me. I gathered the whole of it into a book at eliasodar.com. The book is the long version. If you want it, it is there. I will not mention it again. Let me start with the shelf because everything else follows from it. My grandmother kept a shelf in the back of her kitchen, and on it was every tin, croc, jar, and can that had come through that house and not yet been given a job. A lard tin, a coffee tin with the label worn off. Crocs with a chip out of the rim that could not be trusted for pickling anymore, but held flour just fine. Nothing on that shelf was there because she was saving it for someday. Everything on it was waiting for a job and jobs came along constantly. She would want something to carry seed in and she would go to that shelf. Somebody would need a thing to soak beans in overnight and she would go to that shelf. A grandchild wanted a place to keep buttons or fishing hooks, that shelf. And she kept it in order, which is the part folks miss when they try this and end up with a barn full of clutter instead. Everything on it was clean. Everything was dry. Everything was the right way up. And if a thing sat there a whole year without finding work, it went out. A shelf is a shelf. A pile is a pile. The difference between thrift and hoarding is whether you can put your hand on the thing you want in 4 seconds. I asked her one time why she did not simply throw out the ones with chips in them. And she said, "The worth of a thing is in what it can still do, not in what it used to be." That is the principle and it is the whole of this video. That jug is not garbage. It is a watertight vessel with a handle and there is no shame in it having held milk first. And I would say this too since we are being plain with each other. It is not really about the money. Nobody gets rich saving $5 on a scoop. What it is about is the habit of looking at a thing and seeing what it could be instead of what it was. That habit costs nothing to keep and it pays you in a hundred small places. And once a man loses it, he will buy things all his life that were already sitting in his own kitchen. Now, let me show you the five jobs. Number one, and this is the best of them, deep watering. Here is the trouble with watering from a can or a hose. You stand there and you sprinkle the top of the ground and it looks wet and you feel like you have done something. But most of that water sits in the top inch and is gone by afternoon, drawn off by the sun and the wind before a root ever tastes it. And worse than that, shallow watering teaches a plant to keep its roots shallow because roots go where the water is. Then the first hot dry week comes and that plant has nothing deep to draw on and it wilts while you are standing there with the hose wondering why. What a plant actually wants is water delivered slow and delivered down where the roots live. There is an old test for this that costs you nothing. After you have watered the way you normally do, go back an hour later and push your finger into the soil beside the plant or better dig a small hole with a towel and look. Nine times out of 10, you will find the top inch dark and wet and everything below it dry as a bone. That is the whole problem in one look. And once a man has seen it for himself, he does not go back to sprinkling. So take your jug, poke two small holes low on one side down near the bottom corner. And I mean small. A nail heated on the stove goes through cleanly. Two holes rather than one. So the water goes out both sides of the plant instead of running off in one direction. Set the jug beside the plant an inch or two off the stem and put a couple of stones inside it before you fill it because an empty jug in the wind will go across your garden and you will find it in the fence row. Then fill it with water and cap it loosely or leave the cap off. That water will take hours to go out those two little holes. Hours. It seeps down slow and it goes deep. And the plant drinks it at the rate it can actually use instead of the rate you can pour. One jug on a tomato in July will do more for it than three times that water thrown at the surface. If you want to do it better still, sink the jug partway into the ground when you set your plants in the spring so the holes are down at root depth from the start. Bury it 2/3 under and leave the neck standing proud so you can find it and fill it. Then all summer you walk down the row with a bucket and fill jugs instead of standing with a hose. Do that at planting time rather than later because sinking a jug beside a grown tomato means cutting through roots you spent all spring growing. and put a bit of screen or a scrap of cloth over the mouth if you leave the cap off or you will have mosquitoes breeding in there inside of a week and every one of them will find you while you are working. One jug will serve a tomato, a pepper, an eggplant, or a squash hill. For a young fruit tree, you want two or three of them set around the drip line rather than one at the trunk because a tree drinks from out where the branches end, not from the middle. Now, I will tell you plainly that this is the free version of something much older. Folks in dry countries have buried unglazed clay pots in their beds for a very long time, filled them and let the water weep out through the clay itself. Same idea exactly. Water put down where the roots are and let out slowly. The jug is not as good as the clay pot, but the jug is in your kitchen tonight and it costs nothing. And I would rather you did the free thing today than planned the better thing all summer. Number two, a proper seedling waterer. Anyone who has raised seedlings knows this misery. You have a flat of little things just up, thread thin, and you go to water them, and the water comes out in a gush and knocks half of them flat and washes the soil off the roots of the rest. Take a jug, fill it, and put the cap back on tight. Then, poke a dozen or so small holes in the cap itself. Not the jug, the cap. Now tip it over gently and you get a soft rain instead of a stream. Squeeze the jug a little and it comes faster. Ease off and it slows. It is the gentlest watering you can do and it cost you a jug and a minute with a nail. Keep one of these on the bench by your seed flats and you will use it every day of the spring. It is also the right way to water a newly seated row outdoors where a hose will float your seed right out of the furrow and leave it all bunched at the low end. And it is what you want for anything just transplanted for the same reason. A plant put in the ground yesterday has no grip on the soil around it yet. And a hard stream will wash the dirt away from those new roots and leave them sitting in the air. and the plant will sulk for a week over it or die outright. One small thing that matters, make those holes in the cap smaller than you think. You can always add another hole. You cannot take one back. And loosen the cap a quarter turn while you are watering. Sealed up tight, the jug will glug and stall as the air fights its way back in. And you will get a dribble and then a rush and then nothing. Let a little air in at the top and it pours smooth and even the whole way down. Number three, a scoop. This one takes about 30 seconds and I have half a dozen of them around this place. Leave the cap on and leave the handle where it is. Cut away the wall of the jug on the side opposite the handle. Cutting down at an angle so you leave the bottom and part of the side intact and you take away the top corner. What you are left with is an open mouth, a solid scoop bottom, and that molded handle to hold it by. Now you have a scoop that holds a good deal, weighs nothing, will not rust, and cost you nothing at all. I use them for feed, for grain, for scooping compost out of the bin, for potting soil out of the pile, for wood ash out of the bucket, for sand. One hangs on a nail beside every bin that has something in it worth scooping. When one finally cracks, I make another in half a minute, which is a good deal more than I can say for anything I have ever bought. A word on the cut. Cut it with the cap on and the jug will hold its shape while you work instead of folding in on itself under the blade. Go slow at the corners and run the cut edge over a candle flame for a second or drag a bit of sandpaper along it because a fresh cut in that plastic is sharper than you would expect and it will find your knuckles the first cold morning you reach into a feed bin in a hurry. The garden store will sell you a scoop for several dollars. It is a fine scoop. It is not several dollars better than this one. Number four, a funnel. Cut a jug clean across the middle and turn the top half upside down. And there is your funnel with the neck for the spout and the handle to hold it steady. Cut it a little below the shoulder rather than dead across the middle. And you get a deeper funnel that holds more before it starts backing up on you. I use one for pouring feed into a sack without spilling half of it on the floor. for getting a liquid out of a wide vessel and into a narrow one. For adding water to something with a small opening when you are working alone and you have not got a third hand, a funnel that sits in the neck of a jug and stays there is worth a good deal. And the bottom half you just cut off is not waste either. That is a tray. Set your pots in it to catch drips on a window sill. Use it to hold small tools while you are working. Use it to carry seed heads in from the garden. One jug, two jobs, one cut. And while we are on cutting, there is a sixth use I will throw in for nothing because it takes the same cut and it saves a great many seedlings. Cut a band out of the middle of a jug, a ring four or 5 in tall with no top and no bottom. Push that ring into the soil around a young transplant. So an inch of it is buried and the rest stands up around the stem. That is a collar and it stops cutworms. A cutworm comes along at night and works at the stem right at ground level and fells the plant clean over and you come out in the morning to a healthy seedling lying on its side for no reason. You can see the collar simply puts a wall in the way. It costs nothing and it works. And when the stem has thickened past carrying, you lift the ring off and use it on the next one. Number five, and this is the one folks find surprising. Warmth. Fill your jugs with water, cap them tight, and set them in your cold frame or your greenhouse or your little tunnel tucked in among the plants along the north side. Here is what happens. All day the sun pours in and that water takes up heat. Water holds heat far better than air does and far better than soil. Then the sun goes down and the air in there cools off fast and those jugs start giving back what they took in slow all night long. It will not keep a hard freeze out of a cold frame all by itself. I want to be square with you about that. But on a night that would have just barely nipped your plants, a few gallons of water in there can be the difference between losing them and not. Paint them black or set them where the sun strikes them directly and they take up more. Four or five jugs will do a small frame and come spring you empty them and they go back to being scoops and waterers. Two things to mind with this. Leave a little air space at the top rather than filling them to the brim because water swells when it freezes and a jug filled dead full and frozen hard will split at the seam. And set them along the north side of the frame, not the south, so they are catching the sun without shading the plants you are trying to grow. The same trick works indoors on a window sill in early spring. a jug of water behind your seed flat on the cold glass taking the edge off the night. It is a small thing and it costs nothing and small things kept up are most of what growing anything comes down to. Now, a few things you should know before you start because I would rather tell you than have you find out. First, know what the jug held. milk, water, cider, juice, anything meant to be drunk, that is a food container and it is fine. Something that held a chemical of any kind is not fine. And no amount of washing makes it fine. If you do not know what was in it, it does not go anywhere near your garden or your feed. Second, wash it out right away. A jug that had milk in it and sat 3 days in a warm kitchen is a foul thing, and you will not want it in your hands. Rinse it the day it empties with hot water and a little soap, and let it dry with the cap off. That is the whole difference between a shelf of good jugs and a shelf you do not want to touch. Third, and this is the one people get wrong. I would not use these for storing water you intend to drink. That is not what they are for once they have been in the garden. And milk leaves behind what you cannot see even after washing. Garden water, wash water, stock water, feed. That is their proper work. And fourth, do not fool yourself about how long they last. Sunlight takes these apart. A jug left out in the open through a summer and a winter will go brittle and chalky and crack when you squeeze it. A season or two of hard outdoor use is what you should expect, and then it goes in the bin at last, and you make another from the one that came home Tuesday. That is not a failing. It only means you should not build anything permanent out of them. Now, why has nobody told you any of this? Walk into a garden store and you can buy a watering spike that does exactly what our jug with two holes does, and it will cost you5 or $6 a piece, and you will want a dozen. You can buy a seedling waterer with a fine rose on it. You can buy a scoop, a funnel, and a set of water-filled tubes for your cold frame that do precisely what a gallon of water in a jug does. Every one of those is a decent product. And I am not calling anybody a thief. But there is no scheme needed here and no villain either. The money simply runs the other way. Nobody makes a living telling you the answer is already in your kitchen and you were about to throw it out. So, nobody says it. And a thing nobody has a reason to say gets forgotten inside of one generation. My grandmother did not have a name for what she was doing with that shelf. She would not have called it thrift or ingenuity or any of that. She would have thought it plain foolishness to do otherwise. So this week, before you carry the next one out to the bin, stop and ask the question she taught me. Not what was this, what can this still do. Then tell me in the comments what county you are in and tell me the best use you have found for something you were about to throw out. Not just jugs, anything. I would like to build a list of them because I know very well that between all of you there are a hundred good uses I have never once thought of and that is exactly how these things get saved and passed along.
